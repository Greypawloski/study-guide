## 1. nonforfeiture-options (1:22)

**Script**

Nonforfeiture options. What happens to your cash value when you stop paying premiums?

A permanent policy, like whole life, builds cash value with every premium. Here, a hundred-thousand-dollar policy has built up twenty thousand dollars over fifteen years. Arizona law says that value can't simply be forfeited. If premiums stop, the owner gets three choices.

Option one: cash surrender. The owner takes the twenty thousand dollars and walks away. Coverage ends, and any gain above the premiums paid is taxable.

Option two: reduced paid-up. The cash value buys a smaller policy, here about thirty-five thousand dollars. It's still permanent insurance, fully paid up, so no more premiums are ever due. Covered for life.

Option three: extended term. The face amount stays the full hundred thousand dollars, but it becomes term insurance. It only lasts as long as the cash value can buy, here about eighteen years, then it expires. This is usually the automatic option.

To recap. Cash surrender: get the cash, no more coverage. Reduced paid-up: less amount, for your whole life. Extended term: same amount, for less time. And remember for the exam: if the owner doesn't choose, the insurer usually applies extended term.

## 2. settlement-options (1:14)

**Script**

Settlement options. How the death benefit gets paid to the beneficiary.

The owner can choose a settlement option in advance, or the beneficiary can choose at claim time. The default is a lump sum: paid all at once, and generally income-tax free.

With interest only, the insurer holds the money and pays out just the interest. That interest is taxable. The principal later goes to a secondary beneficiary.

Now the pair everyone mixes up. With a fixed period, you choose how long, say ten years, and the insurer calculates the payment. With a fixed amount, you choose the payment, say two thousand dollars a month, and payments run until the money runs out. Whatever you lock in, the other is calculated.

Life income options pay for the rest of the beneficiary's life. Life only pays the most, but stops at death. Life with period certain guarantees payments for a set number of years. Life with refund returns any unpaid principal. And joint and survivor pays until the second person dies, so it pays the least.

The rule to remember: more guarantees means a smaller payment.

## 3. per-stirpes-per-capita (1:06)

**Script**

Per stirpes versus per capita. What happens when a beneficiary dies before the insured?

John is insured for three hundred thousand dollars. He names his three children: Ann, Ben and Cara. Cara has two children of her own, Dee and Eli.

Then Cara dies before John. Who gets her share?

Per stirpes means "by the bloodline." Each branch of the family gets an equal share. Ann gets a hundred thousand, Ben gets a hundred thousand, and Cara's hundred thousand passes down to her children: fifty thousand each to Dee and Eli.

Per capita means "by the head." The money is split equally among the living named beneficiaries only. Ann and Ben each get a hundred and fifty thousand. Dee and Eli get nothing.

So remember: per stirpes passes a share down the bloodline. Per capita splits it among the survivors.

## 4. buy-sell (1:10)

**Script**

Buy-sell agreements. Cross-purchase versus entity, and how many policies each one needs.

When a business owner dies, their heirs inherit their share of the business. Without a plan, the surviving owners could end up in business with the heirs. A buy-sell agreement says the survivors will buy that share, and life insurance provides the cash to do it.

In a cross-purchase plan, each owner buys a policy on every other owner. With three owners, Ana, Ben and Cruz, that's six policies. The formula is n times n minus one, so four owners would need twelve.

In an entity plan, also called stock redemption, the business itself owns one policy on each owner. Three owners, three policies. The business is the owner, payer and beneficiary, and buys back the shares.

To recap: in cross-purchase, the owners insure each other. In entity, the business insures each owner. Either way, the premiums aren't deductible, and the death benefit is generally received income-tax free.

## 5. policy-types (1:22)

**Script**

Term, whole life, and universal life. How premium, coverage and cash value behave over a lifetime.

Term life covers a set period, here twenty years. The premium is the lowest of the three, but there's no cash value, and when the term ends, coverage ends. Renewing later costs more at an older age. Term gives you the most coverage per premium dollar.

Whole life covers you for your entire life with a level premium that never changes. It builds guaranteed cash value that grows tax-deferred. At age one hundred, or one twenty-one under newer tables, the cash value equals the face amount, and the policy endows.

Universal life is permanent too, but flexible. You can pay more, pay less, or even skip premiums, as long as the cash value covers the monthly charges. Cash value earns a current interest rate with a guaranteed minimum, and you can choose a level or increasing death benefit. Underfund it, though, and it can lapse.

Side by side: term is temporary and cheapest. Whole life is fixed and guaranteed. Universal life is permanent and flexible.

## 6. ul-options (1:04)

**Script**

Universal life: Option A versus Option B.

Option A, also called Option 1, is a level death benefit. Here it stays at two hundred fifty thousand dollars. As the cash value grows inside the policy, it becomes part of that face amount. So the insurer's actual risk, the net amount at risk, shrinks over time, and that makes Option A cost less.

Option B, also called Option 2, is an increasing death benefit. Beneficiaries receive the face amount plus the cash value. The insurer's risk stays at the full two hundred fifty thousand dollars, so Option B costs more.

An easy way to remember it: A is level. B is bigger.

## 7. replacement (1:14)

**Script**

Replacing a policy in Arizona. Who does what, and the deadlines to know.

A replacement is buying a new policy or annuity where an existing one is lapsed, surrendered, borrowed against, reduced or otherwise changed as part of the deal. It's not illegal, but it's closely regulated, because replacing can cost the client new surrender charges, a new contestable period, and higher premiums at an older age.

Here's the producer's job. First, ask about existing coverage. The applicant and producer sign a statement saying whether a replacement is involved. Second, present and read the replacement notice, no later than when the application is taken. Both the applicant and producer sign it. Third, leave the applicant a copy, along with any sales materials used, and send copies to the insurer.

The replacing insurer must notify the existing insurer within five business days, and keep its replacement records for at least five years. The existing insurer must offer the owner information about the old policy.

And the safety net: a replacement policy gets a thirty-day free look, compared with ten days for a normal life policy. Misrepresenting a policy to get someone to replace it is twisting, an unfair trade practice.

## 8. policy-timeline (1:10)

**Script**

The policy timeline, with Arizona's numbers.

It starts at delivery. The owner gets a free look of at least ten days to return the policy for a full refund. Thirty days on a replacement.

When a premium is late, the thirty-day grace period kicks in. Coverage stays in force, and if the insured dies during the grace period, the benefit is paid minus the premium owed.

If the grace period runs out, the policy lapses. An automatic premium loan, if elected, or a nonforfeiture option takes over.

A lapsed policy can be reinstated within three years, with evidence of insurability and back premiums plus interest of no more than six percent. But not if it was surrendered for cash.

After the policy has been in force two years, it becomes incontestable: the insurer can't void it for misstatements on the application. And the suicide exclusion ends two years from issue or reinstatement.

In order: ten-day free look, thirty-day grace, three-year reinstatement at six percent, two-year incontestable and suicide. And watch out: life insurance grace is thirty days, but group conversion is thirty-one.

## 9. beneficiaries (1:14)

**Script**

Who gets paid? Beneficiary rules.

The primary beneficiary is first in line. If every primary beneficiary has died, the contingent beneficiary takes over. And if no beneficiary survives, the proceeds go to the insured's estate, where they can be tied up in probate and exposed to creditors.

Can the owner change the beneficiary? If it's revocable, yes, any time, no permission needed. Most designations are revocable. But an irrevocable beneficiary has vested rights. The owner needs their consent to change them, take a policy loan, assign the policy, or surrender it.

What if the insured and the primary beneficiary die in the same accident, and no one can tell who died first? Under the Uniform Simultaneous Death Act, the insured is presumed to have survived. So the primary is treated as dying first, and the money goes to the contingent beneficiary.

One more rule: minors can't collect proceeds directly. The money goes through a trust, a court-appointed guardian, or a custodian. Naming a trust as beneficiary avoids delays.

## 10. 1035-exchanges (1:00)

**Script**

Ten thirty-five exchanges. Swapping one contract for another without paying tax on the gain.

Life insurance for life insurance: tax-free. Life insurance for an annuity: tax-free. Annuity for annuity: tax-free. But an annuity for life insurance: not allowed. Life insurance or an annuity can also be exchanged for a qualified long-term care policy.

For a clean exchange, keep the same owner and the same insured or annuitant. Any cash you receive, called boot, is taxable. And a ten thirty-five exchange is still a replacement, so the replacement rules apply.

The memory trick: you can move toward an annuity, never away from it. Going from an annuity to life insurance would turn taxable annuity gains into a tax-free death benefit, so the IRS doesn't allow it.

## 11. mec (1:10)

**Script**

Modified endowment contracts, and the seven-pay test.

The IRS sets a limit on how fast you can fund a life insurance policy. During the first seven years, the total premiums you've paid can't exceed what seven level annual premiums would have paid by that point. Stay under that rising limit, and it's a normal life policy.

But pay in too much, too soon, even once, and the policy becomes a modified endowment contract, or MEC. And once a MEC, always a MEC.

What stays the same? The death benefit is still generally income-tax free, and the cash value still grows tax-deferred. What changes is money you take out while living. Loans and withdrawals come out gains first, taxed as income, plus a ten percent penalty before age fifty-nine and a half.

For example: a forty-five-year-old has fifteen thousand dollars of gain in a MEC and borrows ten thousand. All ten thousand is taxable, plus a one-thousand-dollar penalty. The same loan from a non-MEC policy? No tax. And an exam clue: single premium whole life is usually a MEC.

## 12. annuity-phases (1:18)

**Script**

How an annuity works. Pay in, grow tax-deferred, then turn it into income you can't outlive.

Phase one is the accumulation period. Premiums go in, either as a single payment or flexible payments over time, and earnings grow tax-deferred. Take money out early, and you may face surrender charges. An immediate annuity skips ahead: one premium, with income starting within one year. A deferred annuity starts income later.

Phase two is the annuity period. When the owner annuitizes, which is usually irrevocable, the value becomes a stream of regular income, and that income can be guaranteed for life.

Payout options rank just like settlement options. Life only pays the most. Then life with period certain, then refund life, and joint and survivor pays the least. More guarantees, smaller payments.

In Arizona, the annuity free look is ten days, thirty days if the owner is sixty-five or older, and fifteen days if no buyer's guide was given at application. And remember: life insurance protects against dying too soon. Annuities protect against living too long.

## 13. arizona-numbers (1:39)

**Script**

Arizona numbers in two minutes. Every number you need to memorize.

Licensing. You get four exam attempts in twelve months, then wait a year. Apply for your license within one year of passing. Your license lasts four years and ends the last day of your birth month. You need forty-eight hours of continuing education per term, including six hours of ethics. Report address changes and other-state actions or convictions within thirty days. And complete four hours of annuity training before selling annuities.

Policy provisions. A life policy's free look is at least ten days. The grace period is thirty days. Reinstatement is available for three years, with interest of no more than six percent. A policy is incontestable after two years, and the suicide clause also lasts two years. Group life can be converted within thirty-one days, with no evidence of insurability.

Replacement and annuities. A replacement gets a thirty-day free look. The replacing insurer notifies the existing insurer within five business days and keeps records for five years. The annuity free look is ten days, thirty if the owner is sixty-five or older, and fifteen if no buyer's guide was given at application.

Federal and the guaranty fund. Fifty-nine and a half is the penalty age for early MEC and annuity gains. The seven-pay test decides whether a policy is a MEC. Employer-paid group term life is tax-free up to fifty thousand dollars. And the Arizona guaranty fund covers three hundred thousand dollars in death benefits, one hundred thousand in cash value, and two hundred fifty thousand in annuity value.
