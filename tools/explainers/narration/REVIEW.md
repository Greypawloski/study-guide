## 1. Buy-sell agreements
*Section 3, Lesson 3.2 · about 1:20 narrated · 1,206 characters*

Buy-sell agreements. How life insurance keeps a business running smoothly when an owner dies.

When a business owner dies, their share of the business usually **passes to their heirs**. Without a plan, the surviving owners could end up running the company with a spouse or child who never worked there. **A buy-sell agreement** solves that. It's a legal agreement that the surviving owners will buy the deceased owner's share, and **life insurance provides the money** to do it.

In a cross-purchase plan, **each owner buys** a policy on **every other owner**, and is the owner and beneficiary of those policies. With three owners, that's six policies. **The formula** is n times n minus one, so four owners would need twelve policies.

In an entity plan, also called a stock redemption plan, **the business itself** buys **one policy on each owner**. Three owners, three policies. **The business is the owner**, the premium payer and the beneficiary, and it uses the death benefit to buy back the deceased owner's shares.

To recap: in a **cross-purchase plan**, the owners insure each other. In an **entity plan**, the business insures each owner. **Either way**, the premiums aren't tax-deductible, and the death benefit is generally received income-tax free.

## 2. Replacing a policy in Arizona
*Section 3, Lesson 3.4 · about 1:40 narrated · 1,581 characters*

Replacing a policy in Arizona. Who does what, and the deadlines you need to know.

**A replacement happens** when someone buys a new life insurance policy or annuity, and as part of that, an existing policy is lapsed, surrendered, converted, borrowed against, reduced or otherwise changed. Replacement isn't illegal, but it's **closely regulated**, because the client can lose value: new surrender charges, a new contestable period, and higher premiums at an older age.

Here's the producer's job in Arizona. **First, the producer** submits a statement, signed by the applicant and the producer, saying whether the applicant has existing coverage. **Second, if a replacement** is involved, the producer must present and read the replacement notice no later than when the application is taken, and both the applicant and the producer sign it. **Third, the producer** leaves the applicant a copy of the signed notice and any sales materials used, and submits copies to the insurer.

Now the insurers. **The replacing insurer** must notify the existing insurer **within five business days**, and keep its replacement records for at least five years. **The existing insurer** must let the owner know they can get information about the existing policy, and provide it within five business days of a request.

And the consumer's safety net: a replacement policy comes with a **thirty-day free look** for a full refund, **compared with at least ten days** on a regular Arizona life policy. Finally, remember that using misrepresentation to persuade someone to replace a policy with another insurer is twisting, an unfair trade practice.

## 3. Term vs. whole life vs. universal life
*Section 4, Lesson 4.1 · about 1:50 narrated · 1,506 characters*

Term, whole life and universal life. Let's compare how the premium, the coverage and the cash value behave over a lifetime.

**Term insurance is pure protection** **for a set period** of time, here twenty years. It pays only if the insured dies during the term. **It has no cash value**, and when the term ends, the coverage ends. Because it's pure protection, term gives **the most coverage per premium dollar**. And **renewing later costs more**, because the premium is based on the insured's older age.

**Whole life covers** the insured for their **entire life**, with **a level premium** that never changes. It builds **guaranteed cash value** that grows tax-deferred. And **at age one hundred**, or one twenty-one under newer mortality tables, the cash value equals the face amount. The policy endows, and the face amount is paid to the owner.

**Universal life is also** permanent insurance, but it's flexible. The owner can **pay more, pay less**, or even skip premiums, as long as the cash value can cover the monthly charges. The cash value earns a **current interest rate**, with a guaranteed minimum. The owner can **adjust the death benefit**, choosing a level or an increasing option. But if the cash value **runs too low** to cover the charges, the policy lapses.

Side by side: **term covers** a set period, with the lowest premium and no cash value. **Whole life lasts** for life, with a fixed premium and guaranteed cash value. **Universal life lasts** for life if it's funded, with flexible premiums and cash value that earns a current rate with a guaranteed minimum.

## 4. Universal life: Option A vs. Option B
*Section 4, Lesson 4.3 · about 1:00 narrated · 887 characters*

Universal life death benefit options. Option A versus Option B, and why the choice changes the cost.

Option A, also called Option one, **is a level death benefit**. In this example, it **stays at two hundred fifty** thousand dollars for the life of the policy. **As the cash value grows**, it grows inside that face amount, so it's part of what the beneficiary receives, not extra. That means the insurer's actual risk, called **the net amount at risk**, shrinks over time. And less risk for the insurer means **Option A costs less**.

Option B, also called Option two, **is an increasing death benefit**. The beneficiary receives the **face amount plus the cash value**. So **the insurer's risk stays** at the full two hundred fifty thousand dollars, no matter how much cash value builds up. Because the insurer carries more risk, **Option B costs more** than Option A.

An easy way to remember it: **A is level**, and **B is bigger**.

## 5. The Arizona policy timeline
*Section 5, Lesson 5.1 · about 1:50 narrated · 1,525 characters*

The policy timeline. Here are the key provisions in the order you'd meet them, with Arizona's numbers.

**It starts at delivery**. Every Arizona life policy gives the owner **a free look of at least ten days** to return it for a full refund. On a replacement, it's thirty days. Next, if a premium is late, **the thirty-day grace period** keeps the coverage in force. If the insured dies during the grace period, the insurer pays the death benefit, minus the overdue premium. And if the grace period runs out without payment, **the policy lapses**. If the owner elected an automatic premium loan, the cash value pays the premium instead, or a nonforfeiture option takes over.

After a lapse, the owner can **reinstate the policy within three years**, as long as it wasn't surrendered for cash. They'll need to show evidence of insurability and pay the overdue premiums, with interest of no more than six percent a year. Then there are the two-year marks. **Once a policy has been in force** for two years during the insured's lifetime, it becomes incontestable: the insurer can't void it because of misstatements on the application. And **the suicide exclusion** also lasts two years, measured from issue or reinstatement.

**So in order**: a ten-day free look, a thirty-day grace period, three years to reinstate at no more than six percent interest, and two years until the policy is incontestable and the suicide exclusion ends. **One trap** to watch for: the grace period on a life policy is thirty days, but the conversion period on group life is thirty-one days.

## 6. Who gets paid? Beneficiary rules
*Section 5, Lesson 5.2 · about 1:30 narrated · 1,438 characters*

Who gets paid? Beneficiary rules: primary and contingent, revocable and irrevocable, and what happens when the insured and the beneficiary die together.

**Beneficiaries are paid in order**. **The primary beneficiary** is first in line to receive the proceeds. **If every primary** beneficiary has died, the contingent, or secondary, beneficiary receives the money instead. And **if no named beneficiary** survives the insured, the proceeds go to the insured's estate, where they can be delayed by probate and exposed to creditors.

Can the owner change the beneficiary? **With a revocable designation**, yes. The owner can change it at any time, without anyone's permission. **But an irrevocable beneficiary** has vested rights. The owner needs that beneficiary's consent to change the designation, take a policy loan, assign the policy or surrender it.

Now, what if the insured and the primary beneficiary die in the same accident, and no one can tell who died first? **The law presumes** the insured survived the beneficiary. So **the primary is treated** as dying first, and the proceeds **go to the contingent** beneficiary, or to the insured's estate if there isn't one. This rule comes from the **Uniform Simultaneous Death Act**.

One more rule: **minors can't receive** life insurance proceeds directly. The money has to go through a trust, a court-appointed guardian, or a custodian under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. Naming a trust as the beneficiary helps avoid delays.

## 7. Per stirpes vs. per capita
*Section 5, Lesson 5.2 · about 1:10 narrated · 1,086 characters*

Per stirpes versus per capita. These two beneficiary designations only matter when a beneficiary dies before the insured, and the exam loves to test the difference.

**Here's John**. He's insured for **three hundred thousand** dollars, and he names his three children as beneficiaries: **Ann, Ben and Cara**. Cara has two children of her own, **Dee and Eli**.

Now, **Cara dies before John** does. When John later dies, what happens to Cara's share?

If John chose per stirpes, which means by the bloodline, **each branch** of the family gets an equal share. **Ann gets** one hundred thousand, **Ben gets** one hundred thousand, and Cara's one hundred thousand passes down to her children. **Dee and Eli each** receive fifty thousand.

If John chose per capita, which means by the head, the money is **split equally among** the named beneficiaries who are still living. That's just **Ann and Ben**, so they each get one hundred fifty thousand dollars, and **Cara's children receive nothing**.

So, **per stirpes passes** a deceased beneficiary's share down to their descendants. **Per capita splits** everything among the surviving named beneficiaries.

## 8. Settlement options
*Section 5, Lesson 5.3 · about 1:50 narrated · 1,650 characters*

Settlement options. When a life insurance policy pays a death claim, the settlement option decides how that money actually reaches the beneficiary.

The policyowner can choose a settlement option in advance, or leave the choice to the beneficiary at claim time. **The default is a lump sum**: the entire death benefit, **paid all at once**. And a lump-sum death benefit is generally received free of income tax.

With the interest only option, the insurer keeps the principal and **pays the beneficiary just** the interest it earns. Here's the exam point: the death benefit itself is tax-free, but **that interest is taxable**. Later, **the principal goes to a secondary** beneficiary.

Next, the pair the exam loves to mix up. **Under the fixed period option**, you choose how long the payments last, **say ten years**, and **the insurer calculates how much** each payment will be. **Under the fixed amount option**, you choose the size of each payment, **say two thousand dollars** a month, and **payments continue until** the principal and interest run out. So whichever one you lock in, the other is calculated.

Life income options pay for the rest of the beneficiary's life. **Life only pays** the largest amount, but payments stop at death. **Life with period certain** guarantees payments for a minimum number of years, even if the payee dies early. **Life with refund** returns any unpaid principal to a secondary beneficiary. And **joint and survivor** keeps paying until the second of two people dies, so it **pays the smallest amount**.

So remember the trade-off: **the more the insurer guarantees**, the smaller each payment. And for **fixed period versus fixed amount**, whatever you lock in, the other is calculated.

## 9. How an annuity works
*Section 6, Lesson 6.1 · about 1:40 narrated · 1,564 characters*

How an annuity works. Life insurance protects against dying too soon. An annuity protects against the opposite risk: living too long and running out of money.

Every annuity has two phases. The first is the accumulation period. The owner pays in premiums, **either as a single** lump sum or as flexible payments over time, and **the earnings grow** tax-deferred. **An immediate annuity** skips ahead: it's bought with one premium, and income begins within one year. A deferred annuity starts paying later. And **withdrawing money early** from a deferred annuity can trigger surrender charges.

The second phase is the annuity period, also called the payout period. **When the owner annuitizes**, which is usually irrevocable, the accumulated value is **converted into a stream** of regular income payments, and those payments can be **guaranteed for life**.

Payout options rank the same way as life insurance settlement options. **Life only pays** the most each month, but stops at death. **Life with period certain** guarantees payments for a set number of years. **A refund life annuity** guarantees that at least the premium paid comes back. And **joint and survivor**, which pays until the last of two annuitants dies, **pays the least**. More guarantees mean smaller payments.

In Arizona, an annuity's **free look is ten days**, or **thirty days if** the owner is sixty-five or older. And if the buyer's guide and disclosure document weren't provided at or before the application, the free look is **at least fifteen days**. **Remember**: life insurance protects against dying too soon. Annuities protect against living too long.

## 10. MECs and the 7-pay test
*Section 7, Lesson 7.2 · about 1:40 narrated · 1,429 characters*

Modified endowment contracts and the seven-pay test. What happens when a life insurance policy is funded too quickly.

Federal tax law limits how fast a life insurance policy can be funded. It's called **the seven-pay test**. **During the first seven years**, the total premiums paid can't exceed what would have been paid by that point if the policy were paid up with seven level annual premiums. **Stay under that limit**, and the policy is taxed as normal life insurance.

But **pay in too much**, too soon, and the policy becomes **a modified endowment contract**, or MEC. And once a policy is a MEC, it stays a MEC permanently.

So what changes? **Some things stay the same**. The death benefit is still generally income-tax free, and the cash value still grows tax-deferred. **What changes** is money taken out while the insured is living. Loans and withdrawals come out gains first, which is called last in, first out, and they're taxed as ordinary income. Before age fifty-nine and a half, there's generally a ten percent penalty on top.

Here's an example. A forty-five-year-old has **fifteen thousand dollars of gain** in a MEC, and takes a **ten-thousand-dollar loan**. Because gains come out first, **the full ten thousand** is taxable as ordinary income, plus **a ten percent penalty** of one thousand dollars. **The same loan** from a policy that isn't a MEC wouldn't be taxed while the policy stays in force. And **an exam clue**: single premium whole life is usually a MEC.

## 11. 1035 exchanges
*Section 7, Lesson 7.3 · about 1:00 narrated · 927 characters*

Ten thirty-five exchanges. Section ten thirty-five of the tax code lets an owner swap one contract for another without paying tax on the gain.

Here's what's allowed. **Life insurance for another** life insurance policy: tax-free. **Life insurance for an annuity**: tax-free. **One annuity for another** annuity: tax-free. **But an annuity for a life** insurance policy is not allowed. Life insurance or an annuity can also be exchanged for a **qualified long-term care** policy.

For a clean exchange, **keep the same owner**, and the same insured or annuitant. If the owner **receives any cash** in the exchange, called boot, that cash is taxable. And remember that a ten thirty-five exchange is **still a replacement**, so all the replacement rules apply.

Here's the memory trick: you can move toward an annuity, but never away from it. **Going from an annuity** to life insurance would turn taxable annuity gains into a tax-free death benefit, so it isn't allowed.

## 12. Arizona numbers in 2 minutes
*Cheat sheet · about 2:10 narrated · 1,914 characters*

Arizona numbers in two minutes. These are the numbers that show up again and again on the exam.

Licensing. You get **four exam attempts** in a twelve-month period, then you wait a year. After you pass, **apply for your license** within one year. Your license **lasts four years** and expires on the last day of your birth month. Each term, you need **forty-eight hours** of continuing education, including six hours of ethics. **Report a change** of address, or an action against you in another state, within thirty days. And complete **four hours of annuity** training before you sell annuities.

Policy provisions. A life policy's **free look is at least ten** days. **The grace period** is thirty days. You can **reinstate within three** years, with interest of no more than six percent. A policy becomes **incontestable after two** years, and **the suicide exclusion** lasts two years. And **group life can be converted** to an individual policy within thirty-one days, with no evidence of insurability.

Replacement and annuities. A replacement policy gets a **thirty-day free look**. The replacing insurer must notify the existing insurer **within five business days**, and **keep its records** for five years. An **annuity's free look is ten** days, **thirty days if the owner** is sixty-five or older, and **fifteen days** if no buyer's guide was provided at application.

Federal rules and the guaranty fund. Before age **fifty-nine and a half**, a ten percent penalty generally applies to taxable gains taken early from a MEC or an annuity. **The seven-pay test** decides whether a policy is a MEC. Employer-paid group term life is tax-free up to **fifty thousand** dollars of coverage. And Arizona's guaranty fund covers up to **three hundred thousand** dollars in life insurance death benefits, **one hundred thousand in cash** value, and **two hundred fifty thousand** in annuity value.
